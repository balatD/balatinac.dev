Case study · 01 / 04

Four legacy sites, one TYPO3 instance.

A nine-month consolidation into a single TYPO3 v13 platform with shared content elements, role-based editorial flows, and a CI pipeline that quietly handles content sync between staging and production.

Year2025 Duration9 months RoleLead Backend Team3 devs · 2 editors StatusLive

01Context

The client ran four public-facing sites built between 2014 and 2019 — each on a different TYPO3 major, each with its own quirks and its own editor handbook. Three of the four shared more than 60% of their content by volume but had drifted to four slightly different shapes. Editorial cycles were duplicated, redirects were folklore, and security updates landed on whichever site had a developer paying attention that month.

The brief was simple. One platform. One editorial workflow. No data loss. No downtime past office hours.

02The problem

The hard part was never the engineering. It was the politics of four editorial teams who had each spent five years curating their own little fiefdom of templates and "we always do it this way" muscle memory.

  • Four schemas to reconcile, three of which thought they were canonical.
  • ~14,000 content elements with image references pointing at four different storage layouts.
  • A redirect graph nobody had a complete picture of.
  • Editors who, reasonably, did not want to learn a new tool while doing their day job.

The migration was 20% TYPO3, 80% spreadsheet diplomacy.

03Approach

Audit before code

The first six weeks were spent not writing TYPO3 code. We built an extension audit spreadsheet — every plugin, every TypoScript include, every custom backend module — and graded each as keep, port, replace, or delete on sight. About 40% of installed extensions were "delete on sight."

Shared content elements

Instead of porting four sets of templates we collapsed them into one library of ~24 content elements. Each element accepted the same fields no matter which site it was rendered on, and per-site styling lived in a single SCSS partial keyed off the site root. Editors got one handbook.

Blue/green database, one-line cutover

We ran the new platform alongside the old ones for three weeks against a replicated database, mirrored writes through a thin shim, and flipped a single Nginx upstream when the parity report read all-zeros for 48 hours.

# cutover.sh — what it actually came down to
ssh edge-01 "ln -sfn /etc/nginx/upstreams/v13 /etc/nginx/upstreams/active && nginx -s reload"
ssh edge-02 "ln -sfn /etc/nginx/upstreams/v13 /etc/nginx/upstreams/active && nginx -s reload"
# total downtime: 0.4s per edge, no in-flight requests dropped

04Stack & decisions

  • TYPO3 v13 over staying on v11 LTS. The upgrade window only gets harder.
  • PHP 8.3 on FPM, behind Nginx. Boring.
  • MariaDB 10.11 with read replicas for the public site, primary-only for the backend.
  • Docker + Coolify for staging — every merge request gets its own preview URL.
  • GitLab CI for build, test, and the content-sync pipeline that pushes editorial changes from staging to production on a 5-minute schedule.

Things we said no to

No headless frontend. No microservices. No event bus. No "while we're at it, let's also rebuild search." The platform's job was to be the boring foundation everything else could rely on; it earns its keep by not being interesting.

Six months in, the platform handles all four properties on a single application instance with a quarter of the previous combined infrastructure cost. Editorial cycles that used to require three handoffs are a single ticket. The on-call rotation went from "rotating between four people who each knew one site" to "anyone can pick it up because it's the same system."

06Lessons

  • The boring choices compound. Picking the dull option at every fork meant we shipped on time and the runbook fit on one page.
  • Editors are users. The most valuable hour of the project was sitting next to two editors and watching them work.
  • The audit spreadsheet earned its keep. Every "wait, why is this here?" moment six months later had an answer in row 314.
  • Redirects deserve their own project. Treating them as a chore is how you ship a launch with broken Google results.
